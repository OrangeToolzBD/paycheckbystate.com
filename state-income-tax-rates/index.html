Which states have no income tax on wages?

Nine states do not tax wages: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington and Wyoming. The Tax Foundation’s 2026 report counts eight with no individual income tax at all. Washington is the ninth because it taxes only capital gains, not paychecks.

New Hampshire joined the no-tax group when it repealed its tax on interest and dividends as of 2025, according to the same report.

In the table above, all nine show the same take-home pay on a $65,000 salary: $2,092.60 per biweekly check, because only federal withholding, Social Security and Medicare come out. For comparison:

  • Oregon has the lowest take-home in the table at $1,905.75, or $186.85 less per check. Over 26 paychecks that is $4,858.10.
  • North Dakota comes closest to the no-tax states at $2,092.28, just 32 cents less, with a $16,100 standard deduction and a 2.5% top rate.

No state wage tax does not always mean no income tax on your pay. Local income taxes are left out of these figures, and Tax Foundation notes that ten states have county- or city-level income taxes. To see your own check, use the paycheck calculator.

Flat tax vs graduated tax states

A flat tax state applies one rate to all taxable income after its deductions and exemptions. A graduated state works like the federal system, with higher rates on each higher slice of income. In 2026, 15 states use a flat rate and 26 states plus DC use graduated brackets.

Among graduated states, the highest top rates are California at 13.3%, Hawaii at 11%, New York at 10.9%, and New Jersey and DC at 10.75%.

A flat rate is not automatically the lighter one. Deductions matter as much as rates. Illinois charges a flat 4.95% with no standard deduction, and its $65,000 take-home of $1,974.41 is $41.73 below California’s $2,016.14, even though California’s top rate is far higher. Utah is different again: it takes its standard deduction as a credit, shown in the table as $966.

How were these numbers calculated?

Rates, brackets, standard deductions and exemptions come from the Tax Foundation 2026 table, which reflects the law as of January 1, 2026. The take-home column runs each state through the same paycheck engine for a single filer earning $65,000 a year, paid every two weeks, with no pre-tax deductions.

Each check starts at $2,500 gross. Then:

  1. Federal withholding of $216.15, from the IRS Publication 15-T (2026) percentage method for a 2020-or-later W-4, including its $8,600 adjustment for filers who leave the Step 2 box unchecked.
  2. Social Security of $155.00 (6.2%) and Medicare of $36.25 (1.45%), per IRS Topic 751.
  3. State tax: yearly wages minus the state’s standard deduction and exemption, taxed through its brackets, less any exemption credit, divided by 26. California works out to $76.46 per check, which leaves $2,016.14.

Treat the state figure as an estimate. It leaves out local income taxes, state disability and paid family leave deductions, other state credits and each state’s own rules for retirement contributions. Your employer’s payroll follows your state’s own withholding rules, so the amount on your pay stub may differ slightly.

The table is refreshed each January when the new federal tables and state brackets are published. The full formulas are on the methodology page.

Frequently asked questions

Which state has the highest income tax rate in 2026?

California, with a top rate of 13.3% according to Tax Foundation’s table as of January 1, 2026. Hawaii follows at 11% and New York at 10.9%. A top rate applies only to income above the highest bracket threshold, so income below that threshold is taxed at the state’s lower rates.

Did any states change their income tax rates for 2026?

Yes, several cut rates on January 1, 2026, the Tax Foundation reports. Georgia went from 5.39% to 5.19% on January 1, then to 4.99% for all of 2026 under HB 463, signed May 11, 2026 (Office of the Governor), Idaho from 5.695% to 5.3% and Indiana from 3% to 2.95%. North Carolina reached 3.99%, Ohio moved to a flat 2.75%, and Nebraska and Montana lowered their top rates.

Do local income taxes change these numbers?

Yes, where they apply. The table covers state income tax only, and Tax Foundation notes that ten states have county- or city-level income taxes. If your city or county taxes wages, your take-home pay will be lower than the figure shown here. Check your local tax agency for the rate.
