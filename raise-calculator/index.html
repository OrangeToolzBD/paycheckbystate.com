Raise Calculator

A raise calculator converts between a raise percentage, a dollar amount and a new salary. A 4% raise on $60,000 is $2,400, making new pay $62,400, or $92.31 more per biweekly check before tax. Prices rose 3.4% in the year to August 2026 (BLS, September 11, 2026).

How do you calculate a raise percentage?

To find a raise percentage, subtract your old pay from your new pay, divide by the old pay and multiply by 100. Going from $60,000 to $63,000 is a $3,000 raise, and $3,000 ÷ $60,000 × 100 = 5%. To go the other way, multiply old pay by 1 plus the percentage.

The three ways a raise is usually quoted, all for a $60,000 starting salary:

Percentage: 4%$60,000 × 1.04New pay $62,400 (+$2,400)

The same math works for hourly pay. A 5% raise on $20 an hour adds $1.00, for a new rate of $21.00. At 40 hours for 52 weeks that is $2,080 more a year. To switch between hourly and yearly figures, use the salary to hourly calculator.

Raises compound: two 4% raises in a row add 8.16%, not 8%.

How much more is a raise per paycheck?

Divide the yearly raise by the number of paychecks you get. A $2,400 raise adds $92.31 to each check if you are paid every two weeks, $46.15 weekly, $100 twice a month or $200 monthly. Those are gross amounts; taxes take a share before the money reaches your account.

How much of the gross increase you keep depends on your state and W-4. For a single filer in Texas paid every two weeks with a default W-4, moving from $60,000 to $62,400 raises estimated take-home pay from $1,938.08 to $2,012.25 per check, an extra $74.17. The rest goes to federal withholding ($11.08 more), Social Security ($5.72) and Medicare ($1.34), using the IRS Publication 15-T (2026) method and the rates in IRS Topic 751.

Does a raise keep up with inflation?

A raise keeps up with inflation only if its percentage is at least as large as the rise in prices over the same period. The Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers rose 3.4% over the 12 months to August 2026, so a raise below 3.4% in that year bought less than your old pay did.

The figure comes from the Bureau of Labor Statistics CPI release of September 11, 2026, which reports that the all items index “increased 3.4 percent before seasonal adjustment” over the last 12 months.

To compare a raise with inflation, divide (1 + raise) by (1 + inflation) and subtract 1:

  • 3% raise: 1.03 ÷ 1.034 − 1 = about −0.39%. $60,000 becomes $61,800, which buys roughly what $59,768 did a year earlier.
  • 2.5% raise: about −0.87% in real terms.
  • Break-even: $60,000 needs to rise to $62,040 to match 3.4%.

CPI is a national average, so your own costs may have risen faster or slower. Compare a raise with the CPI change for the period it covers.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 3% raise really a raise?

In dollars, yes: 3% on $60,000 adds $1,800 a year. In buying power it depends on inflation. Consumer prices rose 3.4% in the 12 months to August 2026, according to the BLS, so a 3% raise over that stretch was worth about 0.39% less in real terms than the old pay.

How much is a 5% raise on $20 an hour?

A 5% raise on $20 an hour is $1.00, bringing your rate to $21.00. On a 40-hour week that adds $40 a week before tax, and over 52 weeks it adds $2,080 a year, lifting yearly gross pay from $41,600 to $43,680.

Is a 2.5% raise good?

It depends on what prices and pay are doing around you. Against the 3.4% rise in consumer prices reported by the BLS for the year to August 2026, a 2.5% raise over the same period lost about 0.87% in buying power. Compare it with inflation for the period your raise covers.
