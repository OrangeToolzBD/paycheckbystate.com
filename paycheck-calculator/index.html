Paycheck Calculator

A paycheck calculator turns gross pay into take-home pay by subtracting federal income tax withholding, Social Security (6.2% on wages up to $184,500 in 2026, per IRS Topic 751), Medicare (1.45%) and any state income tax. How is take-home pay calculated?

Take-home pay is gross pay minus pre-tax deductions, federal income tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare and state income tax. The tool runs those steps in that order for a single paycheck, using the 2026 IRS withholding tables and each state’s 2026 brackets, then multiplies by your number of paychecks to show the yearly figure.

  1. Gross pay per check. Your salary divided by the paychecks in a year: 52 weekly, 26 every two weeks, 24 twice a month or 12 monthly. Hourly pay is rate × weekly hours × 52 first.
  2. Pre-tax deductions. Traditional 401(k) deferrals and pre-tax health premiums come out first, which shrinks the wages that income tax is figured on.
  3. Federal withholding. The percentage method in IRS Publication 15-T (2026) annualizes your wages, applies your W-4 answers and turns the result back into a per-check amount.
  4. Social Security and Medicare. 6.2% and 1.45% of pay. Employers also withhold an extra 0.9% on wages above $200,000 in a calendar year, whatever your filing status (IRS Topic 751, updated January 20, 2026).
  5. State income tax. An estimate from Tax Foundation’s 2026 state bracket table (published February 17, 2026), after the state’s standard deduction and exemption.

Worked example: $65,000 a year in Texas vs California

On a $65,000 salary paid every two weeks, a single filer with a default W-4 takes home an estimated $2,092.60 per check in Texas and $2,016.14 in California. Federal withholding, Social Security and Medicare are the same in both states, so the entire $76.46 gap is California’s state income tax estimate.

What changes the federal withholding on each check?

Federal withholding moves with your pay frequency, filing status, the Step 2 two-jobs box, dependent credits in Step 3, the Step 4 adjustments and any pre-tax deductions. On the $65,000 single example above, those settings alone move withholding from about $132 to about $383 per check.

The IRS describes Form W-4 as the form you complete “so that your employer can withhold the correct federal income tax” (IRS, About Form W-4). Here is how each answer plays out on that $2,500 check, per the 2026 Publication 15-T method:

  • Married filing jointly instead of single: $132.31 instead of $216.15.
  • Step 2 box checked (two jobs or a working spouse): $383.35. Publication 15-T sends checked forms to a separate “Step 2, Checkbox” column that withholds more from each job.
  • Step 3 credits of $2,200: $131.54. The yearly credit is spread across 26 checks, about $84.62 each.
  • Step 4(a) adds other income to the wages withheld on, Step 4(b) subtracts extra deductions, and Step 4(c) adds a flat dollar amount to every check.
  • A 5% pre-tax 401(k): withholding falls to $201.15. $125 goes into the plan, but take-home drops only $110, to $1,982.60. Social Security stays at $155.00 because 401(k) deferrals are still subject to FICA.

Which states do not tax wages?

Nine states leave wages untaxed in 2026. Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas and Wyoming levy no individual income tax at all, and Washington taxes capital gains income only. Pick any of them and the calculator’s state line shows $0.00.

These groupings come from Tax Foundation’s 2026 state rates table (February 17, 2026). A few details from that source:

  • New Hampshire joined the no-income-tax group after repealing its interest and dividends tax as of 2025.
  • Washington’s capital gains tax became graduated in 2025, with a top rate of 9% on capital gains above $1 million. It does not reach wages.
  • At the other end, California has the highest top rate, 13.3% on taxable income over $1,000,000 for single filers.

This tool does not model state payroll programs such as disability insurance, so check your state agency for any that apply where you work.

What this calculator leaves out

This calculator estimates regular wages for one job. It does not model local income taxes, state disability or paid family leave deductions, state tax credits, after-tax deductions such as Roth 401(k) contributions or garnishments, or how your employer’s payroll handles year-to-date limits. Treat the result as an estimate, not a pay stub.

  • Local taxes. Where a city, county or school district taxes wages, that amount is not included.
  • State quirks. State credits and states that treat 401(k) deferrals differently are not modeled.
  • Bonuses. Supplemental pay is usually withheld differently. The bonus tax calculator covers that.
  • The Social Security cap. The tool spreads the $184,500 cap evenly. A $300,000 monthly-paid salary shows $953.25 of Social Security every month, while a real payroll typically withholds the full 6.2% until wages pass the cap and then stops.
  • New federal deductions. The deductions for qualified overtime and for taxpayers 65 and older run for 2025 through 2028 and are claimed on your return (IRS fact sheet FS-2025-03, updated July 25, 2025). They are not part of this paycheck estimate.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a $65,000 salary per paycheck after taxes?

Paid every two weeks as a single filer with a default W-4, $65,000 comes to an estimated $2,092.60 per check in a state with no wage tax such as Texas. That is $2,500 gross minus $216.15 federal withholding, $155.00 Social Security and $36.25 Medicare. In California the estimate is $2,016.14 after $76.46 of state tax.

Does contributing to a 401(k) cut my paycheck by the full amount?

No. A traditional 401(k) deferral lowers the wages federal income tax is withheld on, so part of the contribution is offset. In the $65,000 Texas example, a 5% deferral puts $125 into the plan each check but reduces take-home pay by only $110, because federal withholding falls from $216.15 to $201.15.

Why doesn’t my actual paycheck match the estimate?

Your employer’s payroll may include items this tool leaves out, such as local income taxes, state disability or family leave deductions, after-tax benefits and rounding. Year-to-date limits like the Social Security wage base can also kick in at a different point. Compare your pay stub line by line to find the gap.
