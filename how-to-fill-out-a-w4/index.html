How to Fill Out a W-4

To fill out a W-4, enter your name, address, Social Security number and filing status in Step 1, then sign Step 5. The 2026 Form W-4 tells you to complete Steps 2 to 4 “ONLY if they apply to you”: multiple jobs, dependents, other income or extra deductions.

What is Form W-4 for?

Form W-4 tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. The IRS Form W-4 page gives that as its purpose. You hand the form to your employer, not the IRS, and it sets withholding only. It does not change the tax you actually owe for the year.

The 2026 instructions say that if too little is withheld you will generally owe tax at filing and may owe a penalty, and if too much is withheld you will generally get a refund.

If you never turn one in, IRS Publication 15 (2026) tells employers to treat you as single with no adjustments. To check the result before you sign, try the W-4 calculator.

Step 1: personal information and filing status

Step 1 asks for your name, address and Social Security number in 1(a) and 1(b), and your expected filing status in 1(c). Filing status matters most: the form says it decides the standard deduction and tax rates used to work out your withholding.

  • Single or married filing separately
  • Married filing jointly or qualifying surviving spouse
  • Head of household, which the form limits to unmarried people who pay more than half the cost of keeping up a home for themselves and a qualifying individual

For 2026, the matching standard deductions are $16,100, $32,200 and $24,150 (IRS, October 9, 2025). If the name on your Social Security card does not match, the form tells you to contact the Social Security Administration at 800-772-1213. It also warns that some credits and deductions require a Social Security number valid for employment.

Step 2: multiple jobs or a working spouse

Complete Step 2 if you hold more than one job at the same time, or if you file jointly and your spouse also works. Otherwise each employer withholds as if its job were your only income. The form offers three options; choose only one.

  1. (a) The IRS estimator. The most accurate route, and the one to use with self-employment income.
  2. (b) The Multiple Jobs Worksheet. Find the yearly amount where the higher-paying job’s row meets the lower-paying job’s column, divide it by that job’s pay periods, and enter the result in Step 4(c) on the higher-paying job’s W-4.
  3. (c) The checkbox. Only for exactly two jobs in total, checked on both forms. It halves the standard deduction and brackets for each job, which works best when the pay is similar.

Whichever you pick, fill in Steps 3 to 4(b) on only one W-4, ideally the highest-paying job’s.

Step 3: claiming dependents

If your total income will be $200,000 or less ($400,000 or less filing jointly), Step 3 lets you claim expected credits so less tax is withheld. The 2026 form uses $2,200 for each qualifying child under 17 and $500 for each other dependent.

A qualifying child must be under 17 at year end, your dependent, living with you for more than half the year, and have the required Social Security number. The $500 credit covers dependents who do not qualify for the child credit, such as an older child or a qualifying relative. You can also add other credits you expect, such as education credits or the foreign tax credit.

Example: two qualifying children put $4,400 in Step 3. Payroll divides that by your pay periods, so on a biweekly schedule withholding drops by about $169.23 per check (Publication 15-T). The trade-off: bigger paychecks, a smaller refund.

Step 4: other adjustments

Step 4 fine-tunes withholding in three ways. Line 4(a) adds income that has no withholding, line 4(b) subtracts deductions beyond the standard deduction, and line 4(c) takes a fixed extra amount from every paycheck. Leave all three blank if none applies.

  • 4(a) Other income: interest, dividends or retirement income, but not wages or self-employment income. Completing it likely removes the need for estimated tax payments on that income.
  • 4(b) Deductions: the result of the Deductions Worksheet. For 2026 it covers itemized deductions plus qualified tips (up to $25,000), the “and-a-half” portion of qualified overtime (up to $12,500, or $25,000 filing jointly), passenger vehicle loan interest (up to $10,000), and $6,000 for each person aged 65 or older, each with income limits. The overtime calculator shows that portion.
  • 4(c) Extra withholding: any additional amount per pay period, including the Multiple Jobs Worksheet result. The form says this lowers your paycheck and raises your refund or cuts what you owe.

Should I claim exempt?

You can claim exemption only if both conditions on the 2026 Form W-4 are true: you had no federal income tax liability in 2025, and you expect none in 2026. If either is false, the exemption does not apply. Consider running the numbers before checking the box.

You had no 2025 liability if line 24 of your 2025 Form 1040 was zero (or less than lines 27a, 28, 29 and 30 combined), or if your income was too low to require filing.

To claim it, check the “Exempt from withholding” box, complete Steps 1(a), 1(b) and 5, and leave everything else blank. No federal income tax will be withheld, and the form warns you may owe tax and penalties when you file your 2026 return. The exemption lasts one year: you need a new W-4 by February 16, 2027.

Frequently asked questions

Do you still claim 0 or 1 on your W-4?

No. The form has not used allowances since its 2020 redesign. The IRS FAQ says allowances were tied to the personal exemption, which current law eliminated, so the form now asks for dollar amounts. If your employer still has a pre-2020 W-4 on file, you are not required to replace it.

How do I decide what to withhold on my W-4?

Compare your expected tax for the year with what your paychecks will withhold, then use Steps 3 and 4 to close any gap. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator does this with your pay stubs and produces a pre-filled W-4. The IRS says it does not ask for your name or Social Security number.

How do I fill out my W-4 to get the most money back?

A refund is simply tax you overpaid during the year, so a bigger refund means smaller paychecks, not lower tax. The form says an amount in Step 4(c) will either increase your refund or reduce what you owe. Whether that trade suits you is your call; the W-4 calculator shows the arithmetic either way.

Why does the new W-4 look so confusing?

It swapped allowance counting for real dollar amounts, which looks like more work. For most people with one job and no dependents, only Step 1 and Step 5 apply; everything else is optional. The extra pages are worksheets for two jobs and itemized or special deductions, and you keep them for your records.
